Xarath — Character & World Background (Draft v1)

The Multiverse Structure

- A **multiversal bubble** is a self-contained cluster of universes. Every universe inside a bubble is itself infinite in space.
- **Bubbles cannot connect to one another.** There is no natural passage between them — this is *why* it's so difficult for Xarath to reach the new multiverse. What he does at the end of the prequel isn't a normal crossing; it's something that should not be possible, which is part of why it costs him everything to do it.
- Within Umbra's home bubble, there is a **Council of Lords** — other beings of Xarath's rank, each holding dominion over some share of the infinite universes in that bubble.
- **Xarath is the strongest of the Council.** Over time, he has taken in more than half of the infinite universes within the bubble — not by conquering all of them outright, but by consuming/absorbing other lords and inheriting what they held.


How His Power Works

- Xarath can consume other beings — Lords, demons, universes, anything — and take in their power. What he consumes doesn't just disappear; it becomes **part of him.**
- This is *not* something he does freely or as a matter of course. There's a restraint on it — rooted in his own history, not an external law.

His Morality — Where the Restraint Comes From

- Xarath's earliest memory is of being **alone** — before any other being existed. He was first.
- When others eventually came, his first instinct was to destroy them. He did — more than once.
- But he came to understand this was wrong. The shift came from two directions at once, and both are true simultaneously rather than either/or:
    - He learned from what he'd done — the guilt of it.
    - He didn't want to be alone again.
- Out of that, a code formed: **he could release what he took.** Because he was the strongest, and because consuming meant absorbing, he had a choice other Lords didn't — he *could* simply erase things from existence if he wanted to. He chose not to. He did not want true nothingness — not for other life, not even for the raw energy of the multiverse itself.
- This is the moral line that makes what he does in the prequel — consuming his own people, his own universe, his own family — such a violation of who he's built himself to be. He's not a being who casually destroys. He's a being who has spent most of his existence refusing to, and the prequel is the one time he breaks that against everything he stands for, because the alternative (the Enders' kind of ending) is worse.
